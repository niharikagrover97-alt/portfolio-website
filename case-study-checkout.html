Reducing checkout abandonment through moderated usability testing

How a 12-participant usability study identified the friction behind a 68% cart-abandonment rate — and shaped a redesign that lifted checkout completion by 23%.

Role Lead UX Researcher
Timeline 6 weeks
Methods Moderated usability testing, analytics review
Team 1 PM · 2 designers · 4 engineers

  • Key insights

Background

The company’s e-commerce funnel showed a 68% cart-abandonment rate — well above the industry benchmark of roughly 55%. Analytics told us where users dropped off (the shipping and payment steps), but not why. The product team had a redesign planned and wanted evidence to guide it rather than guesses.

I was brought in to lead the research, working alongside the design team who would act on the findings in the same sprint cycle.

Research questions

  1. Where in the checkout flow do users hesitate, backtrack, or abandon — and what triggers it?
  2. How do users perceive trust and security cues during payment?
  3. Does the guest-checkout vs. account-creation decision create friction?

Methodology

I ran 12 moderated remote usability sessions (60 minutes each) over two weeks, using the live site with a sandboxed payment environment so participants could complete a real purchase end-to-end.

Participants

  • Recruited via a panel to match the customer base: 8 returning online shoppers, 4 first-time visitors
  • Mixed device split: 7 mobile, 5 desktop — mirroring the 60/40 traffic split

Session structure

  • Contextual warm-up on recent online shopping habits (10 min)
  • Think-aloud purchase task from product page to confirmation (35 min)
  • Retrospective walkthrough of hesitation moments + SEQ ratings per step (15 min)

Sessions were recorded and analyzed through affinity mapping in FigJam; severity ratings were assigned with the design team in a shared synthesis workshop.

Key insights

Insight 01

The forced account-creation step was the single biggest abandonment trigger — 9 of 12 participants hesitated or expressed frustration, and 3 said they would abandon in real life.

Insight 02

Shipping costs revealed at the final step felt like a “bait and switch.” Participants wanted total cost visibility from the cart onward.

Insight 03

On mobile, the payment form’s error handling caused repeated failures: errors appeared above the fold while users were typing below it, so 5 participants never saw why submission failed.

Impact

Findings were delivered as a prioritized issues list with severity ratings, plus a 6-minute highlight reel that the PM later called “the most persuasive artifact of the quarter.” The redesign shipped with guest checkout, upfront shipping costs, and inline form validation.

+23% Checkout completion rate
−31% Payment-step errors
5.9 → 6.5 Average SEQ score

Reflections

Running synthesis with the design team, rather than handing them a report, was the highest-leverage decision of the project — buy-in was immediate because they saw the patterns form themselves.

If I ran this again, I would add an unmoderated benchmarking round before the redesign to get cleaner before/after task metrics rather than relying primarily on funnel analytics.
