Understanding why users abandon a habit-tracking health app

Retention data said users “lost motivation” after week two. A three-week diary study with 16 participants showed the real problem was the app’s mental model — and reframed the roadmap.

Role UX Researcher
Timeline 8 weeks end-to-end
Methods Diary study, exit interviews
Team 1 PM · 1 designer · data scientist

Background

The app helped users build daily health habits — hydration, movement, sleep. Day-30 retention sat at 11%, and the team’s working theory was that users simply “lost motivation.” The proposed fix was more gamification: streaks, badges, push notifications.

Before investing a quarter in that direction, we wanted to understand what abandonment actually looked like in users’ lives. Cross-sectional interviews wouldn’t capture the moment of drop-off, so I proposed a longitudinal diary study.

Research questions

  1. What does the experience of using (and quietly abandoning) the app look like day to day, in context?
  2. What role does the app actually play in users’ existing routines?
  3. Is “lost motivation” the cause of churn, or a symptom of something else?

Methodology

I ran a three-week diary study with 16 new users, recruited within their first week of using the app — the window when churn risk is highest.

Study design

  • Daily structured prompts (2–3 min): what they did, where the app fit in, one feeling word
  • Twice-weekly open prompts with photo/video entries for richer context
  • Signal-triggered prompts when a participant skipped the app for 2+ days — capturing abandonment as it happened
  • 30-minute exit interviews with all 16 participants, including the 7 who stopped using the app mid-study

Analysis

Over 600 diary entries were coded thematically. I mapped each participant’s engagement arc against their diary narrative, then built a journey model of how the app entered — and exited — daily routines.

Key insights

Insight 01

Users didn’t lose motivation — they lost the logging habit. They kept doing the health behaviors but stopped recording them, then felt guilty, then avoided the app entirely.

Insight 02

The app’s mental model was backwards for users: it treated logging as the goal, while users saw logging as a chore in service of the goal. Streak-breaking punished exactly the people who were succeeding offline.

Insight 03

The “comeback moment” mattered most: after missing days, users who saw a guilt-free re-entry stayed; users greeted by a broken streak left for good.

Impact

The findings stopped the planned gamification investment and redirected the roadmap toward effortless logging and forgiving re-entry. The team shipped quick-log widgets, retroactive logging, and replaced broken-streak messaging with progress framing.

11% → 19% Day-30 retention
2.4× Return rate after missed days
1 quarter Of misdirected work avoided

Reflections

The signal-triggered prompts were the methodological win — abandonment is nearly impossible to study retrospectively because users rationalize it. Catching it within 48 hours produced the study’s most honest data.

Next time I would pair the diary data with in-app behavioral logs from day one; we joined them late, and earlier triangulation would have sped up synthesis.
