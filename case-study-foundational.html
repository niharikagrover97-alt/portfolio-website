Mixed methods B2B SaaS Survey + interviews

Foundational research to define personas for a B2B analytics dashboard

Three product teams were building for a user who didn’t exist. A 400-response survey triangulated with 14 in-depth interviews replaced assumption-based personas with evidence-based ones.

Role Lead UX Researcher
Timeline 10 weeks
Methods Survey, IDIs, cluster analysis
Team 3 product teams · 1 data analyst

Background

The analytics dashboard served everyone from startup founders to enterprise data teams, and each product team had its own picture of “the user” — three of them, all different, none grounded in data. Feature debates stalled because there was no shared reference for who decisions were being made for.

Leadership sponsored a foundational study to answer one question: who actually uses this product, and what are they trying to get done?

Research questions

  1. What distinct user groups exist across the customer base, based on goals and behaviors rather than job titles?
  2. What jobs-to-be-done drive daily usage for each group?
  3. Where do current workflows break down for each group?

Methodology

I designed a sequential mixed-methods study: a broad quantitative pass to find behavioral patterns, followed by deep qualitative work to explain them.

Phase 1 — Survey (weeks 1–4)

  • In-product survey to active users covering goals, workflows, tool stack, and frequency of key tasks
  • 412 complete responses across 9 customer segments
  • K-means cluster analysis (with the data analyst) on behavioral variables surfaced four stable clusters

Phase 2 — In-depth interviews (weeks 5–8)

  • 14 sixty-minute interviews, 3–4 per cluster, sampled from survey respondents
  • Workflow walkthroughs via screen share to ground accounts in actual behavior

Phase 3 — Synthesis (weeks 9–10)

  • Persona development workshops with representatives from all three product teams
  • Each persona backed by quant sizing plus interview evidence — no detail without a data source

Key insights

Insight 01

Job title predicted almost nothing. A “marketing manager” and a “data engineer” often used the product identically; clustering by behavior, not role, produced groups the teams could actually design for.

Insight 02

The largest cluster (38% of users) were “report forwarders” — they didn’t analyze data, they packaged it for someone else. No team had been building for them at all.

Insight 03

The power users every team imagined as “the customer” were just 12% of the base, but generated most support tickets and roadmap requests — explaining the skewed picture.

Impact

The four personas became the shared language across product. Roadmap items now name their target persona, and the previously invisible “report forwarder” segment got a dedicated export-and-share workstream the following quarter.

4 Evidence-based personas adopted by 3 teams
412 Survey responses behind the segmentation
38% Of users uncovered as an unserved segment

Reflections

Bringing product teams into synthesis workshops — rather than presenting finished personas — was what made adoption stick. People defend what they help build.

In hindsight I would have shortened the survey: completion analysis showed drop-off after question 18, and three of the cut questions would have strengthened the clustering.
